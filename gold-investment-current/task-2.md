# Task 2: Current Gold Spot Price, Price Trends, and Geopolitical Drivers

## Current Gold Spot Price

As of March 2026, the gold spot price is approximately **$4,509.40 USD per troy ounce**, representing a gain of **+2.51% (or +$110.50)** within the last 24 hours. Gold is traded continuously from Sunday through Friday, 6:00 PM to 5:15 PM Eastern Time (with a 60-minute break), across multiple global exchanges including COMEX (Chicago), London, Hong Kong, New York, and Zurich. The COMEX futures contract (symbol GC) remains the primary price discovery mechanism for global spot prices, with approximately 27 million ounces trading daily. The current spot price sits significantly above the pre-2022 baseline of approximately $1,800 per ounce, reflecting a structural shift in gold valuations over the past four years.

## Price Trends: One, Five, and Ten Year Historical Performance

Gold has demonstrated a powerful uptrend over all time horizons examined. Over the **one-year period** (2025–2026), gold returned approximately **25.5% in USD terms**, continuing its strong momentum. Looking back five years (2021–2026), gold has compounded at double-digit rates, recovering from a temporary pullback in 2021 (−4.3% return) to establish new highs by 2024 (25.5%) and 2025–2026 (ongoing strength near $4,500/oz). The **10-year perspective** (2016–2026) reveals a U-shaped pattern: moderate gains through 2016–2018, followed by a sustained rally beginning in 2019 (18.4% return) that accelerated dramatically post-2022. The pivotal turning point occurred during the Russia-Ukraine invasion in February 2022, when gold surged from ~$1,800/oz to progressively higher levels, establishing support floors that have held through subsequent market corrections.

The most recent bull phase has been exceptional. Gold broke above $2,000/oz in 2022–2023, exceeded $4,000/oz in 2024, and by early 2026 trades near $4,900–$5,000/oz, with intraday spikes reaching $5,350–$5,434/oz during peak geopolitical escalations. Critically, pullbacks during this period—even 15–20% corrections from cyclical peaks—have consistently found support far above pre-crisis levels, confirming the durability of the underlying bull trend. This multi-year rally stands in contrast to the 2013–2015 bear market (−27.3% in 2013) and the modest gains of 2014–2015, highlighting the reversal of sentiment post-2022.

## Geopolitical Drivers

Geopolitical tensions have become the dominant structural driver of gold prices since February 2022, with the Russia-Ukraine invasion catalyzing a persistent "safety-seeking" bid that remains intact through 2026. Each major geopolitical flashpoint—from Israel-Iran missile exchanges and Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping to US-China tensions over Taiwan and the South China Sea—has pushed gold to progressively higher levels. Unlike equities and bonds, which are tied to specific national jurisdictions and monetary systems, gold operates as a borderless, decentralized safe-haven asset with no counterparty default risk, making it the instinctive hedge when investors fear sanctions, currency debasement, or kinetic conflict.

Central bank activity has reinforced retail gold demand, creating a structural "tightness" in the market. Since 2022, central banks in emerging markets and non-Western nations have accumulated over **1,000 tonnes annually**, driven explicitly by fears of sanctions exposure following the freezing of Russian foreign exchange reserves in February 2022. Key accumulators include the People's Bank of China, Reserve Bank of India, Turkey's central bank, and institutions in Poland, Hungary, Brazil, and South Korea. This de-dollarization trend—shifting reserve composition away from US government bonds toward physical gold stored domestically—has created consistent demand independent of retail sentiment swings.

Near-term geopolitical flashpoints remain elevated: the Middle East remains unstable with ongoing Iran-Israel tensions, energy security concerns around the Strait of Hormuz (threatening oil supplies at $120–150/barrel levels), Eastern European risks from the unresolved Russia-Ukraine conflict, and strategic US-China competition over Taiwan. Each crisis triggers gold inflows as institutional and retail investors "sell what they can and not what they want," leading to temporary corrections even as underlying demand remains strong. Empirical analysis suggests that a 100-point rise in the Geopolitical Risk (GPR) index adds 2–3 percentage points to gold returns, particularly when real yields decline. The long-term effects of repeated crises—fiscal deficits, war-related spending, supply chain fragmentation (onshoring), and persistent currency debasement—create sustained tailwinds for gold despite shorter-term interest rate-driven headwinds.
