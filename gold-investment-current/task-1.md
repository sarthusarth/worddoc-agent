# Task 1: Gold Investment Types, Storage Options, and Key Metrics

## Gold Investment Types

Gold investors have access to a diverse range of investment vehicles, each with distinct characteristics, risk profiles, and cost structures. **Physical gold** (coins, bars, and jewelry) represents direct ownership of tangible assets with no counterparty risk, though it carries storage, insurance, and security costs. Investors purchase physical gold from mints, banks, dealers, and retailers at a premium above the spot price. **Gold Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)** are the most accessible modern approach, tracking gold bullion or futures and trading like stocks through any brokerage. These funds typically charge annual fees under 0.30%, offer high liquidity, and eliminate physical custody logistics. **Futures contracts**, primarily traded on COMEX under the symbol GC, allow investors to bet on future gold prices without physical delivery, with standard contract sizes of 100 troy ounces (though smaller 50 and 32.15 ounce contracts exist). These provide leverage but demand rigorous cash management and knowledge.

Additional investment vehicles include **gold mining stocks and mining ETFs** (such as GDX), which offer leverage to gold price movements through company performance and often provide dividend income, though they introduce corporate and geopolitical risks. **Gold mutual funds** pool capital from multiple investors into diversified portfolios containing ETFs, physical gold, and mining equities. **Options** on futures or ETFs provide asymmetric payoff structures for hedging or speculation. **Streaming and royalty companies** redirect production proceeds to shareholders without operating mines themselves. **Bank certificates**, **digital tokens** backed by vaulted gold, **commemorative coins**, and **vaulted savings plans** with incremental purchasing represent additional niche options. The choice among these instruments depends on investor preferences regarding counterparty risk tolerance, liquidity needs, storage logistics, tax implications, and investment time horizon.

## Storage Options

Gold storage solutions vary significantly in cost, security, and accessibility. **Allocated accounts** segregate specifically titled bullion in accredited vaults, meaning investors own identified bars with direct claims to those exact pieces. **Unallocated pools** offer cheaper exposure without specific bars assigned—investors hold claims on gold quantities but not particular pieces, introducing counterparty risk relative to allocated storage. **Private safes** and **bank safe deposit boxes** provide direct investor control but expose owners to fire, theft, and legal disputes over box access. **Professional vaulting services** managed by third-party custodians like Brinks or Loomis offer insured storage with high security standards, though these incur ongoing fees typically ranging from 0.10% to 0.50% annually. Vaulting arrangements may be segregated (dedicated space per client) or pooled (commingled with other investors' holdings), affecting both cost and liability. For ETF and futures-based holdings, storage is managed automatically by the fund or exchange, eliminating investor logistics.

## Key Metrics and Definitions

**Spot price** is the current market price of gold per troy ounce for immediate physical delivery, typically quoted in U.S. dollars. It represents the baseline value from which dealer markups, fabrication premiums, and storage costs are calculated. Spot price discovery happens in global markets, with the world's leading futures contract (COMEX gold) trading nearly 27 million ounces daily across nearly 24-hour trading hours (Sunday through Friday, 6 pm to 5 pm Eastern time with a 60-minute break).

**Volatility** measures the degree of price fluctuation over time, quantified as a percentage and calculated on daily, weekly, or monthly frequencies. Gold volatility is critical for assessing market uncertainty and risk, particularly during geopolitical stress or financial market turbulence. The World Gold Council tracks historical gold volatility alongside major asset classes (equities, bonds, currencies) to illustrate gold's relative stability and defensive characteristics. Gold's volatility profile varies by time horizon and market conditions; it has historically demonstrated lower or negative correlation to equities and bonds, making it valuable for portfolio diversification. Understanding volatility helps investors assess potential drawdowns, hedge requirements, and the suitability of leveraged products (such as 2x leveraged gold ETFs) for their risk tolerance. Investors should monitor volatility to evaluate whether gold allocations meet their volatility budgets and market expectations.
